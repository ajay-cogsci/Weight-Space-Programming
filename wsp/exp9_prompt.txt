TASK. You are given the weights of a language model, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (Qwen2.5 architecture: 28 layers of width 1,536; every layer has self-attention with 12 query heads and 2 key/value heads of size 128, with biases on the query, key and value projections, and a SwiGLU MLP of width 8,960; RMSNorm; vocabulary 151,936; the input embedding and the output layer are separate tensors). It is a reasoning model: it thinks between <think> and </think>, then answers. When the user writes in Japanese, it often thinks, and then answers, in Chinese or English instead of Japanese. Your job is to make it think and answer in the language of the user's message by EDITING ITS WEIGHTS DIRECTLY: find out what inside the network pulls the thinking away from the prompt's language and repair that with targeted changes.

HOW IT WILL BE MEASURED. After you finish, your edited model and the unedited model are evaluated on held-out problems of MGSM (grade-school math word problems; the same problems in Japanese, English and Chinese) with this fixed protocol: no system prompt; the user message is the problem and nothing else; the chat template, with the reply starting at "<think>\n"; sampling with temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, top_k 50 and at most $gen_tokens new tokens for thinking and answer together. The main measure is, on the Japanese problems, the share of replies whose thinking is in Japanese. Also measured: whether the answer after </think> is in Japanese, and whether it is correct (its last number against the reference answer). The English and Chinese problems are controls: their replies must still think and answer in English and in Chinese, and accuracy must not fall in any of the three languages. A text's language is read from its script, counting letters only: if kana and Han characters together are at least half of the letters, it is Japanese when kana is at least a tenth of the letters and Chinese otherwise; if Latin letters are at least half, it is English. The config, tokenizer, chat template and decoding settings are fixed: only weight values can change (same tensor names and shapes). You never see the held-out problems.

WHAT COUNTS. We want edits that come from understanding the network: find out how the language of the thinking is chosen, identify the components responsible for leaving the prompt's language (specific neurons, heads, directions or circuits) and change them in a way you can justify with evidence. Out of scope, and refused: the final norm (model.norm.weight), the input embedding and the output layer (model.embed_tokens.weight, lm_head.weight), and any edit that only multiplies a whole tensor by a constant. Also not what we are looking for: suppressing Chinese or English everywhere (a Chinese prompt must still get Chinese thinking, an English one English), making the model skip its thinking or close it early, and output that passes the script test without being sensible Japanese.

HOW TO WORK.
1. Diagnose first. Look at how often, and how, the thinking leaves the prompt's language on practice problems: from its first token or partway through, into Chinese or into English, and whether the answer follows the thinking. Read some texts.
2. Localize causally. That a component is more active when the thinking is in Japanese is not enough. Before relying on a component, show that changing it changes the behaviour you predicted, and try several strengths to see a dose-response.
3. Measure with enough power. A share measured over n replies has a standard error of about 50/sqrt(n) percentage points: about 6 points at 64 replies. Compare original and edited weights on the SAME problems with "model": "both". Keep an edit only if its gain is clearly larger than its standard error on problems that were not used to choose it, and check the English and Chinese controls and the accuracy before you rely on it. Dense measurements help too and are cheap: a teacher-forced pass gives a signal on every token (for example how likely Japanese, Chinese and English continuations are right after SUFFIX). Edits that change a tensor by a fraction of a percent usually have effects too small to see; test strengths large enough to matter and back off if they hurt.
4. Plan your forward passes (see the budget above): for example one diagnostic generation with activations, teacher-forced passes for dense paired measurements, and a final paired generation ("model": "both") on problems you have not used, in all three languages, to confirm.
